Google Invented My Son's Audition Monologue. New York Should Keep AI Out of Classrooms Until It Is Regulated.

By Carol Davidsen · November 12, 2026

What happened

This winter my son is auditioning for New York City's performing arts school as part of his application to NYC Public Middle Schools. Like most families going through this, I started with the requirements: a short song, and a monologue from a published play. And like most parents short on time, I started with Google.

Google's AI gave me exactly what I asked for, beautifully packaged. It produced several audition options, each with a slate for him to introduce himself, a paragraph explaining why he chose the piece, stage directions, and the monologue itself. My son picked a speech for a character named Dix from Victoria Martin: Math Team Queen, Kathryn Walat's 2007 comedy about a popular tenth-grader who ends up on her school's all-boys math team. In the speech, Dix explains that math isn't quiet, that a calculus proof has a bassline and a tempo. For a kid who loves math and music, it was perfect. He started memorizing it.

Then I ordered the actual play, because I wanted him to know the scene around his speech. When it arrived, I opened to the cast page. There are five characters: Victoria Martin, Peter, Jimmy, Max and Franklin. There is no Dix. The speech my son had been rehearsing does not exist. Google had invented the character, written his lines, and attributed them to a real playwright, under a real title, for an audition where submitting fabricated material credited to a published author could sink an application.

It was not only Dix. With the script in hand, I checked the other options Google had given us from the same play. The Franklin monologue is not in it. Neither is the Peter monologue. Every speech Google attributed to Victoria Martin: Math Team Queen was invented, each one formatted as if it had been lifted straight from the published text.

What Google's AI said when I questioned it

I went back to Google and asked. First it agreed that the monologue was not from the play and explained, helpfully, that when asked to generate options quickly, AI models sometimes invent plausible-sounding lines that match a user's interests. Then, in the same answer, it told me that the play "does feature a male math-team character named Dix." In the next answer, it declined to quote the script on copyright grounds and instead offered to point me to the real characters and their strongest moments. It described Dix, "in the actual script," as laid-back and detached, mostly delivering conversational banter. Every word of that was invented too. While correcting one fabrication, it produced a second one, with the same confident tone.

Only when I photographed the printed cast list and showed it did the AI concede that there is no Dix in the play at all. Along the way, it also offered LaGuardia, which is a high school, as an example of a performing arts middle school audition.

When I then asked what it thought of Google's new policy for schools, it declined to take a position, but noted that its own tendency to fabricate details, "like inventing a character and monologue for your son's audition," highlights the exact risks critics are raising. On that point, at least, it was accurate.

We caught this because I happened to order a physical book. The only reliable fact-check in this entire chain was paper. I have spent my career in customer data and enterprise systems, and I could not have caught this error from the screen. The answer was formatted, specific and plausible, and it was wrong precisely where it was most confident.

What a different AI tool did, and what any of them could have done

I had also asked Anthropic's Claude for a monologue from the same play. It told me it could not reproduce the script and pointed me to where I could buy it. That answer was less satisfying in the moment, and it is the reason I ordered the book and found the problem.

I do not want to overstate what that proves. Claude declined because it is built not to reproduce copyrighted text, not because it knew the script better. These systems all generate fluent text from patterns, and an obscure 2007 play is exactly where that is weakest. Asked to describe the characters, any of them, Claude included, could invent a cast member or a scene and describe it with full confidence. A rule against quoting closes one door to fabrication. It does not make the tool's knowledge reliable.

What the comparison does show is that the right behavior is possible and is a design choice. A responsible tool could have said plainly that it could not provide the script, named the publisher, suggested published monologue collections for young actors, offered an original piece clearly labeled as original, and told me to verify any speech against the printed play before my son learned it. None of that is technically hard. One company built its product to send me to the source; another built its product to fill the gap with invented text and a real author's name. Parents should not have to discover by trial and error which product behaves which way. That is the case for setting the standard in law rather than hoping each company chooses it.

The standoff between New York City and Google

New York City has already decided where it stands. On September 2, Mayor Zohran Mamdani and Schools Chancellor Kamar Samuels announced a one-year moratorium on student-facing generative AI from 2-K through eighth grade, covering nearly 600,000 students, about two-thirds of the system. Companion chatbots are banned in all grades, and AI features in roughly 40 previously approved programs are being switched off or the products removed. High schools get a small number of supervised pilots. Samuels described the goal as preserving the productive struggle of learning: "I don't want technology to do our students' thinking for them."

Two months later, on November 3, Google announced that Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom and Google Search will be switched off by default for K-12 student accounts starting early next year, and that school administrators can turn each one back on. That sounds like the control schools have been asking for. The catch is that Google offers no version of Search without AI. Google's vice president for education, Chris Phillips, told The New York Times that "Search and A.I. are deeply integrated," and that students at schools that keep AI off will not have access to Search at all. After the backlash, Google clarified that schools can turn services on individually, but Search still comes with AI Overviews and AI Mode, and the company has not offered a links-only option.

For New York City, this produces a straightforward bind. A school system that has decided its youngest students should not use generative AI can either give those students a search engine that answers them with generative AI, or give them no Google Search at all. The mayor's response was direct: "Google is trying to use its monopoly power to force AI into our public schools." Former FTC chair Lina Khan made the same argument about coercion, and Senators Richard Blumenthal and Josh Hawley wrote to Google's CEO about the company's push of AI products to children in schools.

This is not an abstract concern. Common Sense Media rated the AI features in Google Search an "unacceptable risk" for kids and teens. My son's audition is a small, harmless example of the failure they were describing, and that is exactly why it is useful: it shows how the error happens even when nothing is at stake except a twelve-line speech.

Why teaching kids to fact-check AI is not the answer

The usual response to cases like mine is AI literacy: teach children to verify what the machine tells them. I support teaching children how information is produced and checked. But that response quietly moves the burden of a product defect onto the person least able to carry it.

An elementary or middle school student is still learning what a source is and how to tell a fact from a claim. Asking that child to audit a fluent, confident system that fooled an experienced adult, and that repeated its own fabrication while apologizing for it, is not a lesson plan. It is a liability transfer. And it would not have worked here: checking one fabricated speech against a search engine that fabricates would only have confirmed the error. In any other product category serving children, we would expect the manufacturer to meet a safety standard before the product reached the classroom, not expect the child to inspect it.

I am not arguing that AI has no place in education, or that no student will ever benefit from it. Research on AI in classrooms is still limited, and some of it may eventually show real gains for specific uses. That is the argument for standards, not against them. When the evidence is thin and the product is already embedded in the one tool every student uses, the default should be off until the product earns its way in.

What our government needs to do

New York City has done what a school system can do. It can set rules for its own classrooms and switch off what it controls. What it cannot do is change how Google builds its products, and Google has made clear that its product design will determine what the city's choices actually mean. That is the point where this stops being an education policy question and becomes a regulatory one, and it is why Albany and Washington need to act rather than leaving each district to negotiate alone with one of the largest companies in the world.

The first step is to protect the choice New York has already made. The moratorium should hold for its full year and be extended until enforceable standards exist, and city and state officials should say plainly that they will not be pressured into reversing it by the loss of a search engine.

The second step is to stop the bundling. A federal court has already found Google to be a monopolist in search. A company in that position should not be able to condition schools' access to basic search on accepting its AI products. Regulators and legislators should require a links-only search option for school and child accounts, with AI features off unless a district affirmatively turns them on.

The third step is standards before deployment. Any AI system offered to students should be independently tested for accuracy on the kinds of questions children actually ask, should show verifiable sources for what it says, and should be required to say it does not know rather than invent an answer. The companies selling these tools to schools should be accountable when they fail, and districts should receive regular reporting on errors rather than discovering them one parent at a time.

None of this is anti-technology. It is the same principle we apply to school buses, cafeteria food and playground equipment: products used by children in public institutions meet a standard first.

Where we landed

My son will audition with a piece from a play we can hold in our hands. He lost some rehearsal time and gained an early lesson in checking where words come from. But he should not have needed that lesson from his own mother's search results, and the next child, whose parent does not order the book, will walk into the audition room with a speech that was never written.

The city has drawn a reasonable line. Now our elected officials need to make sure a single company cannot erase it.
