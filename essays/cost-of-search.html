The Cost of Search: An Old Economics Problem at the Center of AI Strategy

By Carol Davidsen · October 9, 2026

A problem economists named before computers made it worse

In 1961, the economist George Stigler published a short paper called "The Economics of Information." His argument was simple and, at the time, slightly heretical. Classical economics assumes markets clear at a single price because buyers and sellers share the same information. Stigler pointed out that in real markets, nobody has complete information about anything. Prices for the same good vary widely across sellers, and finding the best price costs something: time, effort, money. He called this the cost of search, and he showed that a rational buyer keeps searching only until the expected savings from one more search equal the cost of making it.

Sixty-five years later, this idea is more relevant than it was in 1961, not less. We generate more data than at any point in history, and somehow it has gotten harder, not easier, to know what anything actually costs, what anything is actually worth, or whether the answer we were just given can be trusted. That is no longer a problem for economists to debate in the abstract. It is the problem sitting underneath most AI strategy decisions being made right now, whether or not anyone has named it.

Stigler's model assumed one price per seller. The buyer's job was to find the seller charging the least for it. That assumption is breaking down. Airlines, hotels, and now even fast-food chains are testing prices that vary not just by seller, but by buyer. Delta has confirmed it uses AI, built with a vendor called Fetcherr, to set a growing share of its domestic fares based on a specific customer's booking intent and inferred willingness to pay. The practice drew a formal letter of inquiry from a group of Senate Democrats in July 2025. Wendy's floated AI-enabled dynamic pricing in 2024, then publicly disavowed anything resembling surge pricing after backlash, before the technology had even launched. McDonald's has been reported to use transaction data from roughly 14,000 US locations to recommend prices by location, and critics say by customer; McDonald's disputes that it prices dynamically or in real time. The FTC has an open inquiry into what it calls surveillance pricing, the use of a person's own data to set a price built around what that person specifically will pay, not what the market will bear. When the price is a function of who is asking, no amount of searching gets a buyer to a stable answer. That is not a hypothetical. It is one more reason Stigler's economics is more relevant now than when he wrote it, not less.

Data was never a fact. AI just made that harder to ignore.

Part of why this problem keeps recurring is that we have gotten sloppy about what the word data actually means. The word dates back to the 1640s, when it simply meant a fact given or granted. It does not mean that anymore. What we call data today is rarely a fact handed to us cleanly. It is a reconstruction, assembled from fragments scattered across systems that were never designed to talk to each other, reconciled through a process data engineers call ETL, extract, transform, load, and then governed through an ongoing effort to flag what is wrong, correct what can be corrected, and quietly set aside what cannot. Even a question as simple as a person's current address requires stitching together records from systems that disagree with each other. Multiply that by every fact a company needs to run itself, and it becomes clear why data engineering exists at all. Not because information is hard to store, but because there is no single, authoritative place where all of it already lives.

Until recently, there was one more wrinkle. A huge share of the world's information, videos, contracts, slide decks, recorded calls, was never stored in rows and columns at all. We called this unstructured data, which was always a slightly dishonest name. Language has enormous structure. A contract has clauses. A recorded sales call has a pitch and a set of objections. The information was never actually unstructured. It was structured in a way nothing but a human being could parse, which meant extracting anything useful from it required a person to watch it, read it, or listen to it, and manually write down what mattered.

What changed in 2022 is that large language models became good enough to do that extraction without a person in the loop. That is the real shift, more than any single flashy capability. The cost of search on a huge share of the world's accumulated information collapsed, almost overnight, in much the same way matching two datasets collapsed the cost of search on television advertising prices a decade earlier.

Collapsing the cost of search does not eliminate the problem. It relocates it.

Here is where most conversations about AI strategy go wrong. They treat the arrival of a technology that can parse almost anything as the end of the cost-of-search problem, when it is really the beginning of a new one, in two specific places.

The first is trust. If data was never a clean fact to begin with, and a language model is now synthesizing an answer from dozens of underlying, imperfect sources at a speed no human could replicate, the old question, is this source trustworthy, does not go away. It gets harder to answer, because nobody can watch the model do the work the way an analyst used to watch a spreadsheet. This is exactly why regulators and auditors are increasingly asking not just what an AI system answered, but what it was trained on, what it searched, and whether that underlying information can be reconstructed and defended after the fact. That is a governance instinct with a long history. It is the same instinct that built ratings guarantees into television ad contracts, aimed now at a much larger and far less visible target.

The second is billing, and it has a precedent that rhymes almost exactly. Telephone bills, well into the 2000s, itemized every single call, because the infrastructure delivering the service was built to count usage precisely. As subscription models took over, usage-based billing fell out of fashion, not because usage stopped mattering, but because it was easier to charge a flat fee than to keep proving what each customer actually used. I have advised organizations trying to make that transition in reverse, moving from flat-fee data licensing to usage-based pricing that charges for the value actually delivered. In nearly every case, the hard part was not designing the pricing model. It was that nobody had built the telemetry to prove usage in the first place. The data existed in theory. Nobody had done the work to turn raw logs into something you could put in front of a customer, or a regulator, and defend.

AI is about to hit this same wall, at a much larger scale. Every enterprise negotiating how to bill for AI-driven search, decisioning, or agent activity is going to discover what usage-based data vendors discovered years earlier. The pricing model is the easy part. The infrastructure to measure what was actually searched, used, and acted on is the part nobody built.

I have watched this dynamic most clearly from inside one unlikely industry, in a market that ran on exactly this kind of asymmetric information for decades before anyone fixed it.

You can Google the price of Microsoft stock. You cannot Google the price of a commercial on BRAVO.

That sentence is not a rhetorical flourish. It is a literal, operational fact about the advertising industry, and I spent years working inside it. There is no public exchange for television advertising. Prices for the same thirty-second spot vary by network, by time of day, by who is buying, and by what relationship the buyer already has with the seller. Rate cards exist, but they are not published, they are not standardized, and the advertiser almost never knows whether the price they are quoted reflects the actual value of the audience they are buying, or simply what the seller thinks they can get away with charging.

This is a version of the lemons problem the economist George Akerlof described in 1970, the paper that explained why markets for used cars, and by extension any market where sellers know more about quality than buyers do, tend to degrade toward low trust and low prices unless someone finds a credible way to signal real quality. Television networks try to solve this with ratings guarantees and make-good airings when they fall short. Advertisers try to solve it by relying on agencies who have spent decades learning which sellers to trust. Neither solution closes the information gap. Both just price around it.

In 2012, I was the Director of Data Integration and Media Targeting for the Obama campaign's reelection effort. We had two datasets that, separately, were only moderately useful. One was viewership data from set-top boxes, tracking how tens of millions of households actually watched television, hour by hour, broken down by age, income, and gender. The other was rate card data, the actual prices networks were charging for advertising slots, built up through years of agency relationships. Neither dataset told you much alone. Matched together, hour by hour and network by network, they revealed something almost no one else in the industry could see clearly: exactly which slots were overpriced relative to the audience they actually delivered, and which were underpriced bargains that conventional wisdom overlooked.

We used that matched dataset to buy television advertising differently than campaigns before us had. Instead of assuming prime time on broadcast networks was the only way to reach persuadable voters, we found that cable networks running outside prime time were, for our specific target audience, dramatically more cost-efficient, even though they looked unremarkable by the metrics everyone else was using. The result, by our advertising agency's own accounting, was a $40 to $80 million efficiency gain on a roughly $500 million television budget. That is not a modeling exercise. That is real money, saved by closing a search-cost gap nobody else had bothered to close, because nobody else had bothered to match the two datasets in the first place.

The pattern repeats wherever information is expensive to find and easy to misrepresent

I want to be precise about what actually happened there, because the lesson generalizes far beyond television. The advertising market had three ingredients that made the cost of search unusually high: prices were dispersed rather than uniform, quality was genuinely hard to verify before you bought, and almost nobody had invested in the data infrastructure needed to see through either problem. Whoever did invest in that infrastructure got an advantage that had nothing to do with creative talent or budget size. It had everything to do with knowing something true that everyone else had to guess at.

That is not only a story about television. It is a story about every market where information is asymmetric and expensive to acquire, including the one every business is now trying to navigate: the market for AI-generated answers.

Both problems are the same problem

Regulators asking whether an AI's underlying data can be trusted, and vendors trying to bill customers for how much value an AI actually delivered, are asking the same question from opposite ends. Both are really asking what the system searched, how reliable what it found was, and what finding it actually cost. That is the question Stigler asked about markets in 1961. It is the question I spent years answering about television advertising rates. It is now the central, mostly unaddressed question of enterprise AI strategy.

The organizations that come out ahead in the next phase of AI will not be the ones with access to the best model. Models are converging, and will keep converging. The advantage will belong to the organizations that already know what their data is worth, where it actually came from, and what it costs to find, verify, and act on it. That is the same advantage a small modeling team found in 2012. Both sides spent close to the same amount on television that year. The advantage was never the size of the budget. It was being able to see which of those dollars were being wasted, slot by slot, while the other side was still guessing.
